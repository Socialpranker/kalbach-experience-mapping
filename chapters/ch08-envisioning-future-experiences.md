# Chapter 8: Envisioning Future Experiences

## Core Idea
Design solutions only after developing real empathy through direct contact with people, not by merely "applying" an assumed perspective; once grounded, represent the intended future either as an extension of the current-state map or through complementary techniques — storyboards, scenarios, storylines, design maps, and user story mapping — chosen to fit the team's need and formality level.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Current-state map vs. future-state map**: Current-state maps depict "the world as it exists today" (this book's default output). A future-state map is a separate, full-fledged illustration of the intended experience built the same way as a current-state diagram — but it's time-consuming and often unnecessary. The lighter alternative: fold the future state into the existing current-state diagram, e.g. as a band at the bottom, so the map shows the transition needed and "both cause and cure are captured in one place." Use a separate future-state map only when the future vision is complex enough to warrant its own diagram; otherwise extend the current-state map.
- **Storyboards**: a sequence of illustrated panels (borrowed from filmmaking) representing scene-to-scene flow, each panel a step in the experience with a rough description of what happens. Used to flesh out and "debug" a concept before building a prototype, to put personas in action under real constraints, and to contrast extreme cases (e.g., novice vs. power user) in separate boards. Low floor to start — stick figures suffice; bring in an illustrator later to formalize.
- **Scenarios**: detailed, text-based descriptions of an intended experience written from the individual's perspective — a lower-barrier companion to storyboards since no drawing is required. Used to give personas a voice in motion, explore edge cases, and validate ideas by exposing which concepts are hard to describe as a lived experience.
- **Storylines / narrative arc** (Donna Lichaw, *Storylines*, Rosenfeld Media 2015): apply the classical (Aristotelian) story structure to the design of products and services rather than to storytelling about them. The arc's stages: Exposition (establish context/characters), Inciting Incident (something changes or goes wrong), Rising Action (intensity builds), Crisis (point of maximum friction, no return), Climax/Resolution (the inciting problem resolves), Falling Action (story winds down), End (typically a return to the original state). Process (with Lis Hubert, *UX Matters* 2014): run a workshop with broad stakeholders → draw the user's journey as a narrative arc on a whiteboard → map content needed at each stage → record existing content below it → identify gaps/weaknesses → prioritize into a content strategy.
- **Design maps** (Tamara Adlin & Holly Jamesen Carr, *The Persona Lifecycle*, Ch. 10): simple team-built diagrams of an ideal experience using only sticky notes and a whiteboard, in four color-coded categories — Steps (blue: what the persona does), Comments (green: thoughts/feelings/pain points), Questions (yellow: gaps and assumptions), Ideas (pink: ways to improve the service). Works two ways: asynchronously (posted in a common area, grown organically as colleagues add notes over days/weeks) or synchronously in a workshop — e.g. Kalbach ran breakout groups that each built one map, then rotated groups to add questions to a neighboring map, then rotated again to brainstorm ideas and sketch wireframes on it.
- **Three key types of models** ("Putting It All Together"): a complete description of an experience needs at least one model from each of three groups — (1) Models of individuals (personas, proto-personas, consumer insight maps), (2) Models of context and goals (experience maps: circumstances, jobs-to-be-done, needs, feelings, motivations), (3) Models of future experiences (what solutions look like, for testing/evaluation). More than one per group is possible but risks model proliferation. Example quick/informal chains: Proto-personas → Experience map → Storyboards, or Proto-personas → Design Map. Example lengthier/formal chains: Personas → Mental Model Diagram → Scenarios & Storyboards → Value Proposition Canvas, or Consumer Insight Maps → Service Blueprint → Storylines → Business Model Canvas.
- **User story mapping** (Jeff Patton, *User Story Mapping*, O'Reilly, 2014): counters the loss of the "big picture" that comes from decomposing a product into isolated user stories (format: "As a `<type of user>`, I want `<some goal>` so that `<some reason>`"). Map elements: User types (roles the system serves, listed at top/side), Backbone (the sequence of user activities across the top — comparable to an experience map's chronology, with granular tasks below it), User stories (the body of the map, prioritized and grouped into releases). Process: frame the idea (why, for whom) → map the big picture (chronological flow, including today's pains/joys) → explore (surface stories, sketch solutions, go back to customers) → create a release strategy (slice into releases, starting with the minimum to reach the outcome) → build, measure, learn (track learning against the map continuously).
- **Customer Journey Mapping Game** (Christophe Tallec & Paul Kahn, We Design Services): a facilitated workshop game that uses the journey itself as the mechanism for aligning stakeholders with conflicting perspectives (engineering, business, public policy). Steps: prepare the game (blank journey worksheet with swim lanes, plus cards for possible touchpoints) → select personas ("Whose journey are you going to map?") → set goals for that persona → add touchpoints in experienced order, as a team → reflect (find patterns, gaps, emotional highs/lows, opportunities) → repeat with a different persona or goal. Use when siloed departments need a shared language dominated by no single function.

## Key Concepts
- **Gaining empathy**: developing genuine understanding of a person through deep, direct listening — the prerequisite step, not optional groundwork.
- **Applying empathy**: using already-developed empathy to take a user's perspective while designing; per Indi Young, this cannot substitute for gaining empathy first — "you can't apply empathy until you've developed it by listening deeply to a person."
- **Current-state map**: a diagram of an experience as it exists today; the primary output type this book has focused on.
- **Future-state map**: a full, separate illustration of the intended/ideal future experience, or a band added to a current-state map showing the same thing more cheaply.
- **Storyboard**: an illustrated-panel sequence depicting how an experience unfolds step by step.
- **Narrative arc**: Lichaw's seven-stage story structure (exposition through end) applied to product/service design decisions.
- **Backbone**: the horizontal spine of user activities across the top of a user story map, playing the same role as chronology in an experience map.
- **Model proliferation**: the failure mode of accumulating more visualization models than the three core groups require, confusing the audience instead of clarifying the experience for them.

## Mental Models
- Think of an alignment diagram as a map of the whole landscape, and a storyboard as one specific route through it — reach for a storyboard when you need to pressure-test a single concrete path, not survey the whole territory.
- Use the narrative arc when the goal is to design content or features "as if crafting a story" — map the ideal journey to the arc first, then build the solution to fill it, rather than writing a story about a solution that already exists.
- Use design maps when the team needs fast, low-fidelity, jointly-owned convergence (sticky notes, whiteboard, asynchronous or workshop-driven) rather than a polished deliverable.
- Use scenarios when illustration is too high a barrier but you still need to know whether an idea survives being described as a lived experience — some of the most attractive ideas fall apart once someone has to narrate them from the user's point of view.

## Anti-patterns
- **Applying empathy before gaining it**: acting empathetic — taking a user's perspective, "walking in his shoes" — without first listening deeply to real people produces solutions that miss actual needs and don't match users' mental models. Story: at a prior company, a small team spent two months behind closed doors designing a new event-planning concept with virtually no contact with potential customers. Despite the team's passion, the concept was doomed from the outset — to anyone who had already gained empathy for the target users, its flaws were obvious. The lesson isn't "do big upfront research" but that ongoing, even brief, empathy-building saves time and reduces risk later.
- **The Mr. Potato Head effect**: decomposing a product into isolated user stories without a shared team vision lets mismatched pieces get combined unknowingly — the software equivalent of a Potato Head ending up with Groucho glasses and big red lips. User story mapping's backbone exists specifically to keep the big picture visible while stories are being written.
- **Model proliferation**: stacking more visualization models onto a project than the three core groups (individual, context/goals, future experience) require. It doesn't add insight, it adds confusion for the audience the diagrams are meant to serve.
- **Assuming a shared mental model from a written description**: describing an idea in a document or in your head and assuming readers will picture the same thing. Without externalizing thinking — pictures, index cards, sticky notes — collaborators build different mental pictures from the same words.

## Worked Example
Kalbach needed to visualize a new concept: letting someone snap a photo of a technical problem and request help from a network of experts and friends. Rather than debating the idea in the abstract or jumping straight to a prototype, the team built it as a storyboard in three passes. First, Kalbach sketched a rough panel sequence on an iPad using a drawing app — fast, disposable, easy to redraw. Second, the team iterated on that sequence together several times, adjusting what happened at each step as the concept sharpened. Third, once the sequence held up, they brought in Debora Aoki, a professional comic artist and graphic facilitator, to redraw the panels into a polished, presentable storyboard. The finished artifact did two jobs at once: it laid out the touchpoints and interactions precisely enough to build from, and it carried the human side of the experience — naming the initial problem and showing the emotions of resolution — which a wireframe or flow diagram alone would not. It was used to form the idea internally and to socialize the concept elsewhere in the organization, without ever needing a working prototype to make the case.

## Key Takeaways
1. Gain empathy through direct contact before generating solutions — applying an assumed perspective is not a substitute, and skipping this step is what killed the two-month, closed-door concept in Kalbach's story.
2. Don't default to building a separate future-state map — first consider adding a future-state band to the current-state diagram so cause and transition-to-cure live in one place.
3. Match technique to need: storyboards and scenarios for emotional highs/lows and edge cases; the narrative arc for structuring product/content decisions as a story; design maps for fast, team-owned, low-fidelity convergence.
4. When applying Lichaw's narrative arc, map the ideal journey to the seven stages first, then design features to fill that arc — don't retrofit a story onto an existing solution.
5. Cover at least one model from each of the three groups — individual, context and goals, future experience — and stop there deliberately; more models add confusion, not clarity.
6. In agile/software contexts, use Patton's user story mapping (user types, backbone, user stories, five-step process) to keep the big picture visible and avoid the Mr. Potato Head effect of disconnected features.
7. For multi-stakeholder alignment across silos, a facilitated format like the Customer Journey Mapping Game builds shared language faster than circulating a static document.
