# Chapter 11: Experience Maps

## Core Idea
Experience maps look past any single company's offering to the broader human activity a person is engaged in — separating "the experience" from "the solution" so an organization can see how its products fit into a person's life within a larger ecosystem of providers, artifacts, and channels.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Experience Map — structural elements**: typical elements include some or all of: Phases of behavior, Actions and steps taken, Jobs to be done/goals/needs, Thoughts and questions, Emotions and state of mind, Pain points, Physical artifacts and devices, Opportunities. The set is deliberately freeform — include only what the story needs. When to use: to depict a general human activity within a domain rather than one company's product relationship; the organization may be implicit, absent, or one of several involved. Differs from a customer journey map in three ways: (1) perspective — an individual as goal-driven within a broad system, not "a customer of a specific service"; (2) scope — the holistic, end-to-end experience including actions, thoughts, and feelings, not the path to a transaction; (3) focus — it breaks away from the purchasing decision as the organizing center, even if a purchase appears somewhere in the map.
- **Job Map** (Lance Bettencourt & Tony Ulwick, Strategyn): a universal 8-step structure that any "job to be done" follows as a process — Define, Locate, Prepare, Confirm, Execute, Monitor, Modify, Conclude (Execute is deemed the most critical step from the person's own perspective). When to use: job maps are narrower in scope than experience maps, so the two work hand-in-hand — use an experience map for the high-level ecosystem view, then drill into a specific step with a job map to find innovation opportunities (e.g., drawbacks in speed, variability, or output quality at that step). Ulwick and Bettencourt note the 8-step division is a normalized model, not necessarily how a person actually experiences the activity — adapt it to fit the situation.
- **Workflow Diagram / Swim Lane Diagram**: rows or columns ("swim lanes") represent different actors or system components, showing the chronological flow of tasks, materials, and information between them. When to use: for the mechanical, multi-actor task sequence within a specific phase of a broader experience or job map — the technique sits closer to a service blueprint than to an experience map, and by default omits context and emotion. It can be extended with storyboard panels and experiential detail (as in Yvonne Shek's version) when the team needs to bridge the mechanical flow with the human story.

## Key Concepts
- **Ecosystem thinking**: designing for how a constellation of connected products and services — possibly from multiple providers — fits together and into people's lives, rather than optimizing one stand-alone offering.
- **Dropout point**: a point in an experience map where a negative experience causes someone to abandon the activity, marked with a downward-pointing red line in Bernardo's food-growing map.
- **Positive feedback loop**: a looped path drawn into an experience map showing where engagement reinforces itself and increases over the course of the experience.
- **Moments of truth / critical moments**: emotionally charged, high-stakes points in an experience, surfaced in Kalbach's LexisNexis workshops through team dot-voting.
- **Jobs to be done**: the goal-driven lens (introduced in Chapter 2) underlying job maps — people are goal-driven individuals seeking a desired outcome, and organizational value comes from supporting that outcome.
- **Touchpoint beyond the organization**: an experience map can include artifacts and channels the organization doesn't own (e.g., "Taxis" and "GPS" in the Exploratorium visitor map), since the map's frame is the person's activity, not the company's system.
- **Mapping overload**: the risk that an overly detailed diagram triggers over-analysis rather than alignment.

## Mental Models
- Think of an experience map as **separating the experience from the solution** — use it to shift a team's thinking from features or internal process toward the outcomes people actually seek.
- Use an **experience map** when the activity spans multiple providers, or none is explicitly named; use a **customer journey map** when the story is specifically about one individual's relationship with one company's service.
- Use a **job map** when you need to zoom into a single step already surfaced on an experience map — the two are complementary altitudes, not competing techniques.
- Think of a **swim lane/workflow diagram** as a service blueprint's close cousin — reach for it to document the mechanical actor/task sequence, and only layer in emotion or context deliberately, not by default.

## Anti-patterns
- **Centering the map on the purchase decision**: collapses an experience map back into a customer journey map and defeats the point of looking at the broader human activity beyond any one transaction.
- **Treating the job map's 8 steps as a rigid checklist**: the authors themselves note the sequence "may not be how a person experiences the world" — forcing every activity into all eight steps misrepresents it; modify the framework to fit the situation.
- **Over-detailing the diagram**: pushes the map toward "mapping overload," where stakeholders either get lost in analysis or dismiss the whole artifact as too abstract to act on.

## Worked Example
Case study: "Workflow Diagrams at LexisNexis," by Jim Kalbach.

While at LexisNexis, a worldwide legal-information provider, Kalbach pitched senior leaders on mapping lawyers' workflows across four markets — France, Australia & New Zealand, Germany, and Austria — pitching France's midsized law firms as the pilot. The approach in every market: follow the full lifecycle of a client matter from the lawyer's own perspective.

**Investigation**: In each country he first gathered existing research (e.g., a recent French market-segmentation study) and interviewed internal stakeholders to sketch an approximate workflow. This guided 10–20 on-site customer interviews per country, all recorded, transcribed, and supplemented with photographs for context.

**Illustration**: From the combined internal and external data, Kalbach built horizontal workflow diagrams — horizontal so they could be read at eye level in workshops, and long enough that printouts ran 10–15 feet. He first modeled a high-level map of a client matter's phases (varying by country), then expanded into detailed diagrams tracking three simultaneous actor types — lawyers, secretaries, and other actors — as parallel rows, annotated with pain points, goals, and notes on state of mind and emotion. Personas, weekly-workload charts, and org charts rounded out the picture.

**Alignment**: Each country got a day-long workshop in three parts: (1) *Understanding the lawyer's experience* — breakout groups read through a section of the workflow and used dot-voting to flag the highest-stakes, most emotionally charged moments; (2) *Brainstorming* — mixed-role groups generated new ways to support lawyers at the pain points just identified; (3) *Group presentations* — each team pitched its strongest idea, sketches included, to the full workshop for feedback. Results were later presented to senior international leadership in Montreux, culminating in a mock trial where leaders role-played a fake court case to further internalize the workflow.

**Outcomes**: The exercises built cross-functional empathy — one Australian stakeholder said the diagrams "should be used to onboard new employees." Because every country's diagram followed the same format, the team could compare how law is practiced across markets. Concrete products followed directly: a scannable legal-news format (lawyers spend idle time waiting at court) and a new offering targeting legal assistants, a previously unaddressed segment. The diagrams remained a standing internal reference for customer understanding.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reach for an experience map when the story crosses multiple providers or has no single named organization — its job is to separate the experience from any one company's solution.
2. Don't let the purchase decision anchor the map; that's what distinguishes an experience map from a customer journey map, even when a purchase phase is present.
3. Treat the element list (phases, actions, JTBD/goals, thoughts, emotions, pain points, artifacts/devices, opportunities) as a menu, not a template — include only what the specific story requires.
4. Pair experience maps with job maps: use the experience map for the ecosystem-level view, and Bettencourt & Ulwick's 8-step job map (Define, Locate, Prepare, Confirm, Execute, Monitor, Modify, Conclude) to mine a specific step for innovation opportunities.
5. Keep workflow/swim-lane diagrams mechanical by default (actors × tasks over time); add storyboard or emotional detail only when the team explicitly needs to bridge process with experience.
6. Structure alignment workshops in three moves — understand (dot-vote critical moments), brainstorm, present — as Kalbach's LexisNexis case shows this sequence converting a raw diagram into shipped products.
7. Calibrate diagram detail to the audience: too little invites the "too abstract" critique, too much invites "mapping overload" — both are named failure modes for this diagram type.
