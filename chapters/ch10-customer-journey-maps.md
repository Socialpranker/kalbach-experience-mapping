# Chapter 10: Customer Journey Maps

## Core Idea
A customer journey map (CJM) traces an individual's chronological experience as a customer of an organization, layering motivations, emotions, and moments of truth onto a timeline of phases — not a mere inventory of touchpoints. Its value is strategic: showing how touchpoints combine into a unified whole so an organization can manage the relationship, rather than optimizing each touchpoint in isolation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Customer Journey Map (CJM)**: a chronological, phase-based diagram of one individual's relationship with an organization as a consumer. Structural elements as the author defines them: a chronological spine of **phases** running across the journey (e.g., a purchase decision phase), against which rows/facets are mapped — typically **customer actions, goals and needs, emotions and state of mind, moments of truth, touchpoints, pain points, satisfaction, opportunities for the organization, and delivery on brand promise**. CJMs are "decidedly less formulaic than service blueprints"; the map's creator selects only the facets appropriate to the organization's needs. Use to understand and improve loyalty and the experience of existing customers — how to engage them, deliver value that brings them back, and make services more relevant.
- **Moment Mapping** (Colin Shaw, 2002): the earliest CJM-like technique — an arrow-shaped diagram plotting the phases of the customer experience, paired with a table that derives opportunities for a positive experience from each moment. Established the "timeline/spine with an arrow" visual convention still seen in later CJMs (e.g., Adam Richardson's map).
- **Experience Blueprint** (Lewis Carbone & Stephan Haeckel, 1994): a pictorial representation of the experience "clues" an organization deliberately engineers, together with specifications of what each clue is meant to do. Use when the goal is to plan which sensory/situational cues to design into an experience, not just map perceptions of an existing one.
- **Customer Journey Canvas** (Marc Stickdorn & Jakob Schneider, *This Is Service Design Thinking*): an open, worksheet-style CJM variant built for co-creation. Use in team workshops to have stakeholders audit a journey together; it aligns frontstage and backstage elements — the provider's pre-service actions against customer expectations, and how the provider sustains the relationship after the service encounter.
- **Customer Lifecycle Map**: a distinct, broader diagram type — not a CJM by another name. It covers the entire lifetime relationship between customer and organization in more abstract phases, versus a CJM's single type of engagement. Use when the question is overall brand loyalty and emotional connection to the organization, not one journey (e.g., buying and owning a car as a whole vs. the purchase decision alone).
- **Consumer Decision Journey** (McKinsey & Company): a circular model that replaces the linear conversion funnel. Use when consumers do heavy independent research before buying (see Zero Moment of Truth) and when post-purchase experience feeds directly into the next customer's evaluation criteria — there is no single "top of funnel" where consumers enter en masse.

## Key Concepts
- **Moments of truth**: pivotal points of contact where a customer's perception of the organization is formed (Carlzon's concept — see Chapter 2); CJMs trace how these accumulate across a journey.
- **Touchpoint**: any point of contact between customer and organization along the journey; a CJM analyzes how touchpoints combine rather than optimizing each alone.
- **Persona / customer segment**: a defined customer type used to focus a CJM on one pattern of needs and behavior (e.g., a novice vs. a veteran user of the same service).
- **Innovation-decision process** (Everett Rogers): a model of the phases an individual moves through when adopting something new, built on a persuasion phase governed by five perceived factors: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability.
- **Perceived characteristics**: Rogers' insight — adoption factors like "relative advantage" exist in the customer's perception, not as objective properties of the product — mirrored in how CJMs seek to understand an offering as customers actually perceive it, not as it objectively is.
- **Zero Moment of Truth**: the pre-purchase online research phase (detailed in a Chapter 2 sidebar) that motivated McKinsey's shift from the funnel to a circular decision journey.
- **Frontstage / backstage**: the split (used in the Customer Journey Canvas) between what the customer directly experiences and the provider's internal preparatory and follow-up actions.
- **Emotional curve**: a line plotted across a journey's phases tracking the customer's emotional state — shown as a prominent central element in Effective UI's broadband-provider CJM.
- **Market continuum model**: John Jenkins' 1972 customer-lifecycle diagram, one of the earliest comprehensive lifecycle models, built on Rogers' innovation-decision process and prior 1960s advertising-response models (DAGMAR; Lavidge & Steiner).

## Mental Models
- Use a CJM when the goal is understanding and improving the experience of *existing* customers across an end-to-end relationship — not when you just need to fix one touchpoint.
- Use a Customer Journey Canvas when you need broad team and stakeholder buy-in and co-creation in a workshop setting, rather than a finished, polished deliverable.
- Think of customer lifecycle map, CJM, and service blueprint as three focal lengths on the same subject: lifecycle is the wide shot (overall brand loyalty, years), CJM is the medium shot (one engagement, e.g., a purchase decision), service blueprint is the close-up (one specific service encounter's operational detail).
- Use the consumer decision journey (circular) instead of the funnel when customers do substantial independent research before purchase, such that one customer's post-purchase experience becomes the next customer's evaluation input.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a CJM as a touchpoint inventory**: the author states directly that CJMs "aren't mere inventories of touchpoints" — omitting motivations, emotions, and moments of truth produces a map that shows *where* contact happens but not *why* the journey feels the way it does.
- **Acting on staff's assumed pain points instead of researched ones**: in the Meridian Health case, internal staff believed scheduling was the primary source of friction; research showed scheduling was actually a strength. Acting on the unverified assumption risked "improving" — and thereby breaking — a process customers were already happy with, while leaving the real problem (lack of guidance) unaddressed.
- **Treating CJM, customer lifecycle map, and service blueprint as a strict hierarchy**: the author is explicit that the relationship among the three "is not strictly hierarchical" and that practitioners frequently use the terms interchangeably in the field — imposing a rigid taxonomy misrepresents actual practice.

## Worked Example
Meridian Health wanted to boost loyalty and referrals among its Radiology patients, and brought in Heart of the Customer (HotC), led by "Mapper-in-Chief" Jim Tincher. HotC worked with Meridian's VP of Marketing and Director of Experience Marketing to define customer segments (personas) and build CJMs exploring what each type of patient thought and felt at every step of the healthcare experience.

The project began with a hypothesis, sourced from internal staff, that scheduling difficulties were the main cause of customer frustration. Research — patient journaling exercises and more than half a dozen focus groups — overturned it: patients found scheduling and registration straightforward, praised the staff, and were satisfied with treatment itself. The real problem, surfaced through moments of truth, was that patients often didn't know what to expect at each stage and weren't proactively guided from step to step.

Two contrasting personas made the finding concrete. "Seen-it-all Stanley," a heavy healthcare user, is unfazed by minor friction — he knows to allow extra time for parking and roughly when results will arrive, so small inconveniences don't dent the loyalty Meridian has already earned; the task there is simply to sustain the existing service level. "Newbie Natalie," a first-time patient, is more anxious, so the same objective friction (parking trouble, not knowing when results will arrive) lands much harder, even though she too is satisfied with her actual treatment. Because Natalie won't stay a "newbie" for long, her first impression determines whether she returns as a future Stanley — making her experience Meridian's biggest opportunity.

HotC moved from insight to action by running workshops with Meridian to brainstorm and prioritize fixes for Natalie's journey. The team concluded better communication was the lever, and Meridian built clearer, more specific guidance at each step to reduce her anxiety and set realistic expectations. Had Meridian instead followed staff's original hypothesis and reworked scheduling and registration, the effort would likely have been wasted — or worse, would have disrupted a process patients already liked — while leaving the actual guidance gap untouched.

## Key Takeaways
1. Structure a CJM as a chronological spine of phases with only the facets relevant to your question layered beneath it (actions, emotions, moments of truth, touchpoints, pain points, satisfaction, opportunities, brand promise) — CJMs are deliberately not formulaic.
2. Validate assumed pain points with real research (journaling, focus groups) before acting on them; internal staff hypotheses about what's broken are frequently wrong and can lead you to "fix" something customers already like.
3. Segment personas by prior experience/sophistication (e.g., first-timer vs. veteran) — identical objective friction produces very different emotional impact depending on what the customer already expects.
4. Look specifically for moments of truth where customers lack guidance about what happens next; uncertainty, not objective service failure, can be the dominant driver of dissatisfaction.
5. Use a CJM to unify touchpoints into one coherent strategy, not to optimize touchpoints one at a time.
6. Match the diagram to the question's scope: CJM for one engagement or decision, customer lifecycle map for the whole brand relationship, service blueprint for one service encounter's operational detail.
7. When the goal is cross-functional buy-in rather than a finished artifact, use an open canvas format (Customer Journey Canvas) instead of a polished CJM.
