# Chapter 7: Align: Designing Value

## Core Idea
A diagram is a means to an end, not the end itself: its purpose is to spark the discourse that aligns an organization around a shared, outside-in view of the experience. At this point the mapmaker's role switches to facilitator, and the primary vehicle for that discourse is a structured group event called the **Alignment Workshop**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Alignment Workshop**: the chapter's central framework — a three-part session: **Empathize** (gain an outside-in view of the individual's experience) → **Envision** (imagine a future that provides meaningful value) → **Evaluate** (articulate ideas quickly and test them for immediate feedback), closed by a final **Plan Experiments** step. Run it after the diagram (Ch6) exists, with 6–12 stakeholders, to convert a static artifact into design decisions.
- **Rules of Divergent Thinking**: go for volume; withhold judgment; build on ideas ("Yes, and…" instead of "But"); find alternatives; encourage crazy ideas; be visual. Apply these explicitly when opening the Envision phase, before any prioritization.
- **Remove Barriers**: identify what blocks people from getting value using four barrier types — Access, Skill, Time, Money (Table 7-1) — plus emotional/social barriers, then ask "how might we overcome obstacles?" at each diagram stage. First directed ideation exercise in Envision.
- **Challenge Industry Assumptions** (via Luke Williams, *Disrupt*): state the unwritten rule with the formula "Everyone in the &lt;industry or category&gt; knows that &lt;assumption&gt;…", then twist it three ways — **Invert** (do the opposite), **Deny** (remove it entirely), **Scale** (make the scarce abundant, or vice versa). Williams calls these assumptions *clichés*. Second directed exercise in Envision, for disruptive rather than incremental ideas.
- **The Ask** (Michael Schrage): a single question — *who do we want our customers to become?* — posed individually at each major division of the diagram, then answers are collected, the best is chosen per division, and solutions are brainstormed toward that transformation. Third directed exercise in Envision; use it to reach for identity-level value instead of incremental feature value.
- **Feasibility-vs-Value Matrix**: a 2×2 grid — feasibility (ease of implementation) on one axis, value to the individual on the other. Sort all ideation output into the four quadrants, then prioritize within each quadrant. Opens the Evaluate phase; the "easy and valuable" quadrant should surface ideas implementable immediately, with no extra funding.
- **Hypothesis Statement**: fixed three-part template — "We believe that providing [solution, service] for [individual, customer, user] / Will likely result in [desired outcome, assumed effect] / We will know this when we see [result, measurable impact]." Use in Plan Experiments to turn a selected concept into a falsifiable, measurable bet before building anything real; no metric means no testable hypothesis.
- **Facilitator's Three Roles**: **Prepare** (invite 6–12 stakeholders, assign roles — facilitator, designers, test moderator, outside experts, senior stakeholders — book an offsite room, plan logistics and an agenda, hold a pre-workshop meeting a week out), **Run the Workshop** (set the stage, engage with the diagram, facilitate divergent thinking, create artifacts, select concepts, run tests), **Follow Up** (survey participants, update the diagram, distribute materials, make diagrams visible, hold weekly checkpoints on assigned experiment owners).

## Key Concepts
- **Empathy (workshop sense)**: not something the facilitator already has, but a state the whole organization must be brought to — seeing the experience through someone else's eyes, spread via the diagram as a conversation hub.
- **Moments of truth**: points in the experience most critical to individuals, surfaced by dot-voting during the Assess step and discussed when breakout groups' ratings diverge.
- **Design studio**: an ideation exercise where each participant individually sketches a possible solution to a given barrier, then the sketches are shared and consolidated as a group.
- **Divergent thinking / Convergent thinking**: divergent = "going wide," generating volume of ideas without filtering (Envision); convergent = narrowing and prioritizing what was generated (Evaluate). The workshop enforces a hard transition between the two.
- **How might we…? / What if…?**: two ideation prompts — the first shifts attention from current to future state, the second changes direction and digs deeper once initial ideas stall.
- **Market experiment techniques**: lightweight ways to test a hypothesis post-workshop — explanatory video, landing page ("fake storefront"), prototype testing, concierge service (manually deliver a simulated service to a small signup group), limited product release (ship one or two features only).
- **Single- vs. multi-day workshops**: the Empathize/Envision/Evaluate structure has no fixed time frame; multiday sessions let the team run two or three rounds of testing with external subjects within the workshop itself.

## Mental Models
- Think of the diagram as **a prototype of an experience** — it lets team members put themselves in the user's shoes, and like any prototype, its value is realized by testing it against people, not by admiring it.
- Think of the workshop as **a campfire, not a presentation** ("Visualizations act as a campfire around which we gather to tell stories" —Al Shalloway): passive viewing defeats the purpose; the diagram must stay open for writing, correcting, and storytelling.
- Use divergent mode when the group is generating (go wide, defer judgment); switch to convergent mode only once, deliberately, when the group starts prioritizing — collapsing the two early caps idea volume before it reaches non-obvious concepts.
- Use "how might we…?" to move from current-state analysis to future-state ideation; reach for "what if…?" when the group's ideas stall and need redirection.

## Anti-patterns
- **Presenting the diagram instead of opening it up**: keeping it as a polished, hands-off artifact for passive consumption blocks the write-on-it, tell-stories, foster-discussion activities that actually generate empathy — a workshop is not a presentation.
- **Filtering ideas during divergent thinking**: judging, narrowing, or over-detailing ideas while still in Envision breaks the "withhold judgment" and "build on ideas" rules and collapses the volume needed to reach genuinely new concepts.
- **Treating workshop concepts as finished requirements**: storyboards, wireframes, and prototypes produced in one or a few days are hypotheses, not specs; skipping Plan Experiments means shipping unvalidated bets as if they were validated ones.
- **Designing a transformation people don't want**: Segway (asks users to become "a weirdo on a scooter") and McDonald's "Supersize Me" (asks customers to become "unhealthy") show that commercially or technically successful ideas still fail when The Ask's answer is undesirable — check the transformation before pursuing the concept, not after launch.
- **Letting the workshop be the end point**: without a post-session survey, an updated and redistributed diagram, and weekly checkpoints against named experiment owners, the alignment and momentum generated in the room evaporates — "alignment doesn't stop with the diagram or with the workshop."

## Reference Tables

Table 7-1: Types of barriers that prevent individuals from getting value

| Barrier | Example | How to identify |
|---|---|---|
| Access — experiences limited to specific times or places | Mobile phones gave access to telephoning on-the-go; smartphones extended this to internet/data from anywhere | Look for instances where an individual cannot consume the product/service at all — are they locked out of value entirely? |
| Skill — people lack the ability to perform a necessary task | Computing before 1970 required trained users until the GUI and mouse (1982); photography before the Kodak camera required training | Many steps in a process signal a skill barrier — how can tasks be made simple enough for anyone to complete? |
| Time — interacting is too time-consuming | Before eBay, buying/selling collectibles was prohibitively time-consuming | Look for high drop-out rates in a process and assess whether lack of time is the root cause |
| Money — people lack the financial means | Airline travel before 1970 was only for the wealthy | Identify points where the service has high cost — how might you offer that same value for free? |

Table 7-2: Transformations from The Ask (positive and negative examples)

| Product/Service | Started as | Transformed the customer into |
|---|---|---|
| Kodak | Camera | Photographers |
| Google | Search Engine | Expert Researchers |
| eBay | Trading Platform | Entrepreneurs |
| Segway (failure case) | New Vehicle | Weirdo on Scooter |
| McDonald's Super Size (failure case) | Value for Money | Unhealthy person |

## Worked Example
Note: this range's actual case study is Jim Kalbach's own **"Rapid Online Mapping and Design Workshop"** sidebar (the Christophe Tallec / Journey Games case study referenced in the assignment brief is confirmed, via full-text grep, to live in Chapter 8, not Chapter 7 — it is reconstructed there instead).

At MURAL (a cloud-based virtual whiteboard company), Kalbach ran a 1.5-day alignment workshop in Buenos Aires with eight participants in mixed roles, to examine and improve MURAL's own product onboarding experience — using MURAL itself as the mapping surface.

**Part 1, Empathize**: before the session, Kalbach pre-built one mural combining three elements: a customer value chain (actors and their relationships), three proto-personas derived from that value chain (Sophia, a design lead, as the primary persona), and an experience map built from prior team-collaboration research plus recent customer interviews (circular shapes marked repeat behaviors). The group discussed each element together, updating personas live as they talked.

**Part 2, Envision**: the group brainstormed barriers to repeat use, prompted by "what is keeping Sophia from using our service repeatedly?", recording answers directly beneath the experience map, then clustering and dot-voting them using MURAL's built-in voting feature. They then ran a design studio: for each identified barrier, participants individually sketched a solution, photographed it, and uploaded it to a second mural for the group to review.

**Part 3, Evaluate**: after lunch, the team split into two groups, each consolidating sketches into one testable solution. Using Usertesting.com (unmoderated remote testing), they got feedback overnight and had results the next morning — some assumptions were validated, others disproven. They iterated on the designs and closed with a concrete implementation plan for the following months.

**Outcome**: the full cycle — understand, prototype, test — ran in under two days with no written proposals or reports. Because the work lived in a persistent online tool rather than static documents, the team could keep updating it and loop in people who weren't in the room, making the mapping effort ongoing rather than a one-time event.

## Key Takeaways
1. Once the diagram exists, your job shifts from mapmaker to facilitator — the diagram's purpose is to spark conversation, not to stand as a finished deliverable.
2. Run the workshop as three enforced modes — Empathize, Envision, Evaluate — with a hard, explicit switch from divergent to convergent thinking between Envision and Evaluate.
3. Force ideation with structured techniques rather than open brainstorming alone: Remove Barriers, Challenge Industry Assumptions (invert/deny/scale), and The Ask, in that order.
4. Prioritize with the feasibility-vs-value matrix before investing in detailed articulation; ship the high-value/high-feasibility quadrant immediately, route the rest to further design.
5. Close every concept with a three-part hypothesis statement and a named lightweight experiment (video, landing page, prototype test, concierge service, or limited release) — workshop output is a belief to test, not a requirement to build.
6. Logistics decide whether facilitation works: 6–12 participants, explicitly assigned roles, an offsite room, and a pre-workshop meeting a week before the event.
7. Momentum requires deliberate follow-up — survey the session, update and redistribute the diagram, and hold weekly checkpoints against assigned experiment owners, since alignment doesn't stop with the workshop.
