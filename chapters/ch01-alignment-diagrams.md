# Chapter 1: Introducing Alignment Diagrams

## Core Idea
Alignment diagrams are a category of diagrams — service blueprints, customer journey maps, experience maps, mental model diagrams, and spatial maps — that visualize value creation by placing an individual's experience and an organization's offerings side by side, joined at their points of interaction. This reframes mapping work as **value-centered design**: the goal is not picking the "correct" diagram type but revealing where individual and organizational value intersect.

## Frameworks Introduced

**The 7 Principles of Alignment** — criteria for what makes a diagram a genuine alignment diagram rather than a generic UX artifact. Apply them both when building a diagram and when auditing a finished one.

- **Principle of Holism**: An alignment diagram frames human behavior within its larger ecosystem, not as research into one specific product. How: capture what people do, think, and feel in context — not just their use of a single system. Apply when scoping a project, to resist narrowing to one software workflow.
- **Principle of Multiplicity**: A true alignment diagram layers several facets of information at once — this simultaneity is what "alignment" technically means. How: on the individual side, track actions, thoughts, feelings, state of mind, goals, and pain points; on the organization side, track processes, actions, objectives, metrics, and the actors or roles involved. Apply when designing the diagram's rows or layers — a single-facet timeline isn't sufficient.
- **Principle of Interaction**: The diagram must expose touchpoints and the context around each one, so the layered information comes together to show an actual exchange of value. How: done well, the diagram becomes a walkable prototype of the experience that can be traced touchpoint by touchpoint. Apply when defining the boundary layer between the two halves.
- **Principle of Visualization**: Value comes from a single graphical, all-at-once overview of behavior and process — the same content as a written report or slide deck loses its impact once linearized. Apply when the goal is making an abstract concept like "user experience" tangible to an audience.
- **Principle of Self Evidence**: A finished diagram should need little or no explanation — someone should be able to walk up and orient themselves quickly. How: this doesn't happen automatically from choosing a visual format; it takes deliberate editing down to the most salient points and a cohesive story. Apply during final synthesis, not just layout.
- **Principle of Relevance**: The diagram must speak to real organizational issues, which requires first understanding the organization's goals, challenges, and future plans so the result fits existing thinking. Apply before and during stakeholder scoping.
- **Principle of Validity**: The diagram must be grounded in actual investigation — direct contact with real people through research and observation — rather than assembled from assumptions in isolation. Apply as a non-negotiable input gate before diagramming begins.

## Key Concepts
- **Alignment diagram**: any map, diagram, or visualization that shows both sides of value creation — an individual's experience and an organization's offerings/processes — in one overview.
- **Value-centered design**: a design perspective, coined by service-design expert Jess McMullin, built around "a story about an ideal interaction between an individual and an organization" and what each side gains from it.
- **Value creation**: a bi-directional exchange located where an individual's experience meets an organization's offering — the individual receives a benefit (a job done, a problem solved, an emotion experienced), the organization captures worth in return (money, time, attention).
- **Touchpoint / line of interaction / center line / midpoint**: the diagram-specific name for the boundary layer where individual and organization exchange value; every archetype has some version of it.
- **Archetype**: one of the five core alignment-diagram patterns — service blueprint, customer journey map, experience map, mental model diagram, spatial map — against which most related diagram types can be categorized.
- **Towers / goal spaces**: in mental model diagrams, thematic clusters of an individual's tasks, feelings, and philosophies (towers), further divided into specific goals (goal spaces) such as "Choose Film."
- **Alignment attrition**: Jon Kolko's term for a team's natural tendency to drift out of sync as individual opinions shift over time; a shared visual model counters it by freezing a thought in time.

## Mental Models
- Think of an alignment diagram as two mirrored halves — individual experience on one side, organizational offering on the other — hinged together by an explicit interaction layer.
- Use an alignment diagram when the goal is shared understanding and cross-department dialogue; don't reach for one expecting an immediate, prescriptive answer.
- Think of value as relational rather than resident in the product or the customer alone — per Drucker's framing, business purpose itself is defined by creating a customer, which only happens at the intersection of the two.
- Use the five archetypes as reference patterns, not a rigid taxonomy — real-world diagrams (and the terminology practitioners use for them) routinely blur the lines between types.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fighting over terminology**: arguing whether something is "really" a journey map versus an experience map wastes effort — practitioners use these terms inconsistently, and the diagram's value comes from its content and the conversation it sparks, not its label.
- **Diagramming a single product or workflow in isolation**: violates the Principle of Holism — charting how someone clicks through one piece of software isn't an alignment diagram; the frame must be the person's broader context.
- **Building the diagram from a conference room instead of the field**: violates the Principle of Validity — without direct contact with real people, the diagram is fiction dressed as insight.
- **Treating the diagram itself as the deliverable**: alignment diagrams don't hand over immediate answers or ready-made solutions on their own; mistaking the artifact for the solution squanders the dialogue it's meant to provoke.

## Worked Example
Kalbach walks through a customer journey map (Figure 1-4) built for an international architect-search service — a real project, anonymized. Structure: across the top run chronological phases of the relationship, from "Become Aware" through "Renew/Upgrade" — the diagram's story spine. Directly below, several rows capture the individual side per the Principle of Multiplicity: actions, state of mind and feelings, desired outcomes, and pain points — parallel facets, not a single action trace. A middle row lists the primary touchpoints, the literal interaction layer. Below that, the organization side takes over: key departmental activities that support or respond to the customer at each phase, followed by a SWOT (strengths/weaknesses/opportunities/threats) analysis. Read top to bottom, the diagram physically embodies Holism (individual context framed above), Multiplicity (several stacked facet-rows per side), and Interaction (a dedicated touchpoints row binding the two halves) in one artifact.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't start by picking a diagram "type" — start by identifying where individual experience and organizational offering need to be aligned; the five archetypes are patterns, not a checklist.
2. Structure every alignment diagram as two halves joined by an explicit interaction layer (touchpoints, line of interaction, center line, or midpoint with arrows).
3. Capture multiple simultaneous facets per side (Principle of Multiplicity) — a single action trace without feelings, pain points, or organizational roles isn't yet an alignment diagram.
4. Ground the diagram in real research contact with people (Principle of Validity) before treating it as authoritative.
5. Edit hard for the Principle of Self Evidence — a visual format alone doesn't guarantee clarity; cut to the most salient points.
6. Treat the diagram as a conversation-starter, not a deliverable — its business value is the shared big picture, broken silos, and surfaced opportunities it produces, not the artifact itself.
7. Expect the diagram to stay valid for years: because it's grounded in relatively stable human needs and emotions rather than volatile product details, it doesn't need frequent rebuilding.
