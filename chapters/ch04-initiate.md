# Chapter 4: Initiate: Starting a Mapping Project

## Core Idea
A mapping project succeeds or fails on how it's framed at the outset: the mapmaker sets the right level of formality, wins stakeholder buy-in, decides what to map and why, selects the appropriate diagram type, and — for formal efforts — commits direction, resources, and cost to a short written proposal before any research begins.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Initiate** (Phase 1 of the book's four-phase process: Initiate → Investigate → Illustrate → Align): the up-front framing phase, before research or drawing. Concrete checklist: (1) determine the level of formality; (2) convince decision makers — know objections, provide evidence, find a champion, run a pilot, create a pitch; (3) decide on a direction — identify the organization's strategy/objectives, determine which experiences to map via a customer value chain, create personas or proto-personas, decide how many diagrams are needed; (4) select the diagram type; (5) define the effort — estimate duration, assess resources, estimate costs, write a proposal.
- **Level of formality (three dimensions)**: plotted across (a) horizontal: single product → service ecosystem, (b) vertical: user-interface design → holistic experience design, (c) group size. Formality should rise moving up and to the right. Use before starting anything, to calibrate how much process to apply: "Only do as much work as needed to work."
- **Customer Value Chain**: "a depiction of key actors and the flow of value to individuals." Process: (1) list all actors/entities involved; (2) place the primary actor and primary provider in the center, provider to the left; (3) place other actors/entities around them by relationship; (4) reorder to show how value flows from providers to customers. Use early (minutes, not days) to scope which relationships to map, pick a diagram type, and set stakeholder expectations. Distinguished from generic "stakeholder maps"/"ecosystem maps" by its explicit focus on value flow.
- **Proto-Personas** (term coined by Jeff Gothelf, *Lean UX*): personas built from a team's existing domain expertise and gut feeling in a brainstorming workshop, not from user research. Built on a four-square grid: name & sketch; demographic/psychographic details; behaviors & actions; needs & pain points. Use when full research-based personas aren't yet feasible; upgrade to real personas once investigation (Ch5) is done.
- **Diagram-type selection (Table 4-2)**: compares Service Blueprint, Customer Journey Map, Experience Map, Mental Model Diagram, and Spatial Map along the five elements from Chapter 2 (point of view, scope, focus, structure, uses). Use after the value chain and personas are set, to match the diagram to the specific relationship and audience being addressed.
- **Proposal structure** (8 elements, ≤2 pages): Motivation, Aim, Goals, Project participants, Activities/deliverables/milestones, Scope, Diagram type, Assumptions/risks/constraints. Use for formal efforts as the negotiable written statement of intent submitted to project sponsors.

## Key Concepts
- **Level of formality**: how rigorous and detailed a mapping effort should be, driven by product-vs-ecosystem scope, UI-vs-holistic-experience design, and team/group size.
- **Customer value chain**: a simple concept map of the actors/entities in an experience and the direction value flows between them.
- **Persona**: a narrative description of a user archetype reflecting common patterns of behavior, needs, and emotions, grounded in actual data (not creative writing).
- **Proto-persona**: a research-free, assumption-based stand-in persona built quickly from a team's existing domain knowledge.
- **Champion**: an influential stakeholder identified early to back and advocate for the mapping effort.
- **Pilot effort**: a small-scale trial map, or an add-on to existing research (e.g., a usability test), run to demonstrate value with first-hand results.
- **Current state diagram**: a visualization of an experience as it exists today — this book's primary focus, distinct from an envisioned future-state layer (Ch8).
- **Proposal**: a short written statement of intent for a formal mapping project, subject to negotiation with sponsors rather than approval on first submission.

## Mental Models
- Think of the level of formality as a sliding scale, not a switch: as scope moves from single product toward service ecosystem, design moves from UI toward holistic experience, and the group grows, formality should rise to match.
- Use a customer value chain when you need, in minutes, to decide which relationships in a complex ecosystem to map, which diagram type fits each one, and where to set stakeholder expectations about scope.
- Use proto-personas as placeholders when there's no time or budget yet for research-based personas — the team upgrades them once real investigation happens.
- Treat stakeholder conviction as a sales problem, not a documentation problem: pair known objections with counter-evidence, a champion, and a first-hand pilot result, since stakeholders typically only see mapping's value after a process is already complete.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating mapping as a one-off deliverable**: diagrams "are not a give-and-go deliverable" — they require internal stakeholders' active participation throughout; skipping this starves the effort of access and buy-in.
- **Applying one fixed formality level regardless of context**: forcing a rigorous formal process onto a small startup effort, or a casual approach onto a large multi-touchpoint ecosystem, wastes effort or under-delivers.
- **Pitching without anticipating objections**: going in without counters to "we don't have time/budget" or "we already know this" lets predictable pushback stall the project before it starts.
- **Conflating customer journey maps, experience maps, and service blueprints** just because all three are chronological: picking the wrong one delivers the wrong point of view for the audience.
- **Getting hung up on diagram-type labels** instead of on how the diagram will show value alignment and drive stakeholder conversation.
- **Multiplying diagrams beyond necessity**: creating a separate diagram for every minor segment instead of only where experiences differ significantly.

## Reference Tables

**Table 4-1: Typical objections to getting started**

| Objection | Error | Argument |
|---|---|---|
| We don't have time or budget. | Creating diagrams takes long and is expensive. | Mapping needn't be costly — a formal project can run a few weeks, about the cost of a usability test or marketing survey. |
| Each department has its own process map. | Functional silos work efficiently individually. | Fine, but do they show interaction across channels and touchpoints? Great experiences cross department lines. |
| We already know all of this. | Implicit knowledge is enough. | Good start — but making it explicit keeps conversations going, preserves insight when people leave, and ramps up new hires. |
| I was in that target group; just ask me. | Inside-out view of customers rather than outside-in. | Your input gives an initial hypothesis; we supplement it with a grounded external perspective — that's where growth insight is found. |
| Marketing already does research. | Marketing and experience research are treated as the same. | Good, but not enough — we need to uncover unmet needs and unexpressed feelings in the context of the overall experience. |

**Table 4-2: Comparison of diagram types**

| Type | Point of View | Scope | Focus | Structure | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Service Blueprint | Individual as recipient of service | Concentrated on service encounters/ecosystems, often real-time | Service provision: roles, backstage actors, processes, workflows, physical evidence | Chronological | Front-line staff, internal teams, managers improving/brainstorming a service |
| Customer Journey Map | Individual as loyal customer, often deciding to purchase | Awareness → purchase → leaving/returning | Cognitive & emotional states; moments of truth, satisfaction | Chronological | Marketing, PR, sales, account mgmt, support, brand managers |
| Experience Map | Individual as actor in a broader activity | Defined by the specific experience/context | Behaviors, goals, jobs to be done; actions, thoughts, feelings, pain points | Chronological | Product managers, designers, developers, strategists — product/service innovation |
| Mental Model Diagram | Individual as a thinking/feeling human in a domain | Breadth of experience, emerges from data | Fundamental motivations, feelings, philosophies | Hierarchical | Same as above — empathy, product/service strategy |
| Spatial Map | Individual within a multifaceted system of interaction | Defined by size/capabilities/constituents of an org | Flow of information and relationships between system components | Spatial | Managers, content specialists, employees — process/information optimization |

## Worked Example
Kalbach builds a customer value chain for a news magazine (Figure 4-3). Following the four-step process: he lists the actors — journalists, publishers, advertisers, stores, readers; places the primary actor (reader) and primary provider (publisher) in the center, provider to the left; arranges the remaining actors around them by relationship; then reorders so value visibly flows left to right — journalists supply content to publishers, publishers monetize that content via advertisers, stores distribute the physical magazine to readers, and readers in turn constitute the audience advertisers are paying to reach.

The finished chain exposes several distinct relationships bundled inside one ecosystem, each calling for a different diagram type: the publisher–store relationship is backstage and logistical, suited to a service blueprint; the reader–advertiser relationship centers on an individual weighing attention/purchase decisions, suited to a customer journey map; the journalist's relationship to producing content is broader human behavior, suited to an experience map. The chain also does scoping work before any research starts: if the publisher actually wants insight into magazine distribution to stores, but the mapmaker had scoped the reader–advertiser relationship instead, the mismatch surfaces immediately — cheaply, in a diagram that took minutes to sketch, instead of after weeks of the wrong research.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fix the level of formality first — driven by product-vs-ecosystem scope, UI-vs-holistic-experience design, and team size — and don't over- or under-build the process relative to it.
2. Before pitching, prepare counters to the five common objections (Table 4-1); pair them with outside evidence, a champion, and a small first-hand pilot, since stakeholders typically only recognize mapping's value after seeing a finished result.
3. Build a customer value chain (list actors → center primary actor/provider → arrange relationships → reorder for value flow) early — it takes minutes and drives scoping, diagram-type choice, and research recruiting.
4. Use proto-personas (Gothelf's four-square grid) as a fast, research-free stand-in when full personas aren't yet feasible; upgrade later via Chapter 5 investigation.
5. Choose diagram type by matching the relationship at hand to Table 4-2's five dimensions (point of view, scope, focus, structure, uses), not by picking a label first.
6. Create a separate diagram per segment or relationship only where experiences differ significantly — don't multiply diagrams needlessly.
7. For formal efforts, write a short (≤2-page) proposal across the eight elements (motivation, aim, goals, participants, activities/milestones, scope, diagram type, assumptions/risks/constraints), and expect to negotiate it rather than get it approved on first submission.
