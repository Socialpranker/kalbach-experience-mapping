# Chapter 13: Spatial Maps and Ecosystem Models

## Core Idea
Spatial maps and ecosystem models are the book's non-chronological, non-hierarchical diagram types: meaning lives in *where* information sits on the page and how layers relate to each other, not in sequence or rank. Both aim for an ecological, systemic view of people's experiences — a broad web of interaction rather than a single path — with John Snow's 1854 cholera map standing as the founding proof that a simple, well-layered map can reveal causality that finer instruments miss.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Spatial maps — structural approach**: A structure type where placement on the page, not time or rank, carries the diagram's meaning; neither chronological nor hierarchical. Layering is the primary technique for showing alignment — a base map of actors and touchpoints gets additional facets (e.g., current organizational capabilities) overlaid on top in distinct colors, so gaps and redundancies become visible and drive discussion. Use when the goal is a holistic, at-a-glance view of relationships across a system, not a sequence of events.
- **Consumer insight maps** (Kim Erwin, Illinois Institute of Technology Institute of Design): an information-dense format built on cartographic principles — Erwin names zones, elevations, topographies, and blueprints as the map conventions she draws on. In practice the map is divided into named zones (in her published example: mindset, activities, anxieties, attitudes, product opportunities), each holding subgroups that add depth. Purpose: preserve the messy, tangled texture of consumers' lives while still making it systematically examinable at both micro and macro levels. Use when stakeholders need emotional contact with the research and persistence of the consumer's voice, not just a simplified summary.
- **Isometric projection**: a specific type of spatial map that renders 3D objects or ecosystems in 2D by rotating all line angles equally, creating a consistent sense of a plane (often reinforced with light shading for a 3D illusion). The plane can stay implicit (angle consistency alone) or go explicit, drawn as colored regions the designers of one case study call *carpets*, each representing a distinct part of the system (e.g., one department). Use to give a service ecosystem a literal "place" feel and to support several different overlays (content flow, research activity, web access) on one stable base diagram.
- **Ecosystem models**: diagrams giving a holistic view of an individual's experience that deliberately go beyond any single organization's own touchpoints — they uncover and document the dynamics of the experience by starting from the job the individual is trying to get done in general, then bringing together whichever services (from one provider or many) serve that job into one picture. A common layout is concentric circles: center = the core actor relationship, each ring outward = a wider circle of relationship (passengers, other cars or products, other services, communities, society, the earth), sliced radially by who/when/where/what/why/how. Use to design or audit a service in the context of the whole system it lives in, not just the company's own channels.
- **Gigamap** (contributed by Peter Jones, OCAD University sLab): in Jones's own words, a Gigamap is "a large-scale visualization of the systemic relationships within a complex domain." It can look like an infographic at first glance, but it is actually a time-intensive team construct grounded in qualitative research — built through stakeholder and expert engagements plus iterative studio critique that evolves the underlying system theory explaining the observations. Because the method takes time to learn, Gigamaps are taught as a studio practice rather than produced as one-off deliverables. Use for complex social or civic domains needing long-term, multi-sector vision-building, not for a single product or service ecosystem.

## Key Concepts
- **Spatial map**: a diagram whose meaning depends on the physical or metaphorical placement of information on the page rather than on time sequence or hierarchy.
- **Layering**: overlaying additional facets of information (capabilities, data, flows) on top of a base spatial map to expose gaps, redundancies, and new relationships.
- **Consumer insight map**: Kim Erwin's information-dense, cartography-inspired format for presenting consumer research in named zones that support both overview and detail.
- **Zones**: Erwin's named regions of a consumer insight map (e.g., mindset, activities, anxieties, attitudes, product opportunities) that group related findings.
- **Isometric projection**: a 2D drawing technique for 3D objects or systems using equal, rotated line angles to imply a consistent plane.
- **Carpets**: colored plane-areas in an isometric map that make the implicit plane explicit, each standing for a distinct organizational unit or system part.
- **Ecosystem model**: a diagram giving a holistic, cross-organization view of the dynamics around an individual's job or experience.
- **Gigamap**: a large-scale, research-grounded visualization of the systemic relationships within a complex domain, developed iteratively as a studio practice.
- **Systemigram**: a compact inset diagram used within a Gigamap to represent one key systemic issue or narrative thread (e.g., access, privacy, open data in the governance case).
- **Three Horizons model**: a foresight framework tracing the progression from today's tensions to a long-view preferred future, used to structure a Gigamap's "waves of change" zone.

## Mental Models
- Use a **spatial map** when the point is showing how things relate to each other in a system, not what happens in what order — location on the page carries the meaning.
- Think of **layering** as the spatial map's equivalent of a chronological diagram's swimlanes: it's how you show alignment, by stacking organizational reality on top of the experience base map.
- Use an **ecosystem model** when the relevant unit of design is the whole job a person is getting done, not the touchpoints owned by one company.
- Use a **Gigamap** when the domain itself is a complex, multi-actor social system (e.g., governance, infrastructure) needing a long-term, foresight-oriented view — not when an ordinary service or product ecosystem map (like the FIAT example) already fits.

## Anti-patterns
- **Expecting sequence or emotional nuance from a spatial or ecosystem map**: these structures deliberately drop chronology and typically omit emotional detail; pair one with a chronological diagram (as in the peer-to-peer car-sharing example, which stacks a spatial map on top of a customer journey) when timing or feelings matter.
- **Treating a Gigamap as a quick infographic deliverable**: it looks like one but is actually a slow, iterative, team-built research artifact; skipping the studio-based critique cycle and stakeholder engagement undermines the system theory the map is supposed to embody.
- **Running spatial-map creation as a large group workshop**: spatial maps are comparatively hard to build collaboratively and can take a long time to produce well; plan for focused authorship and synthesis rather than expecting a room to co-draft one live.
- **Calling any theme-clustered, color-coded layout a "map"**: one case in the chapter (Collaboratonia) is flagged by the author himself as closer to an affinity diagram than a true spatial map — physical placement plus color coding must actually encode relationships, not just group similar items, to earn the spatial-map label.

## Worked Example
**John Snow's 1854 cholera map.** Before Louis Pasteur's germ theory, the dominant explanation for disease was miasma — bad air. When cholera broke out in Soho, London, physician John Snow suspected the real vector was water instead. Microscopic examination of water samples was inconclusive, so Snow turned to mapping: he plotted cholera cases alongside streets, houses, and — critically — water pump locations across the neighborhood. Layering these facts on one map surfaced a pattern no microscope had caught: cases clustered tightly and predictably around a single water pump. Snow used the map to argue for shutting that pump down, and the outbreak's subsequent decline is credited with launching modern public health practice.

Kalbach reads this as an early instance of alignment thinking: the water utility's service, the pumps as touchpoints to that service, and Soho households as the individuals affected were all connected on one diagram — showing that a decision about water treatment and storage made miles away directly shaped outcomes for specific people in central London. The lesson for practitioners: a few well-chosen data points, correctly located and layered on a simple map, can generate a testable hypothesis and reveal causality that more sophisticated but unmapped instruments miss entirely.

**Case study — Gigamapping Canadian Governance (Peter Jones).** A multi-year (2014–2015) research effort at OCAD University's Strategic Innovation Lab (sLab), under PI Evert Lindquist (University of Victoria), engaged stakeholders through a Canada-wide workshop, small-group sessions eliciting influence maps from 35 civic innovators, and an Ottawa symposium — all to understand the future of digital-era governance. The resulting Gigamap organized findings into three zones: inset systemigrams at the top for key citizen issues (access, privacy, open data); a spatial metaphor in the middle for the current government ecosystem, its future challenges, and a transformation vision; and, at the bottom, waves of change structured by the Three Horizons foresight model, tracing today's tensions toward a long-view preferred future. The team's framing was explicit: the project wasn't about "digital governance" as a topic but about the systemic shifts digital culture forces across federal, provincial, and local governance — and the Gigamap served as the shared artifact for building that long-term, multi-sector perspective.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reach for spatial structure when the message is about relationships and system-level location, not sequence — chronology is explicitly not what this structure is for.
2. Use layering as the primary alignment technique in a spatial map: build a base map of the experience, then overlay organizational facts (capabilities, data, flows) in distinct colors to expose gaps and redundancies.
3. Borrow cartographic conventions (named zones, consistent spatial grammar) to keep a dense research map comprehensible at both macro and micro levels, as Kim Erwin's consumer insight maps do.
4. Use isometric projection when a literal landscape feel helps, and use explicit carpets when the plane itself needs to carry meaning (e.g., one carpet per department).
5. Scope an ecosystem model to the whole job a person is getting done, not to one organization's own touchpoints — the value is in showing services the organization doesn't own but that shape the experience anyway.
6. Combine a spatial or ecosystem map with a chronological diagram when both the systemic overview and the sequential, emotional story matter — don't force one structure to do both jobs.
7. Budget a Gigamap as an iterative, studio-based research process with real stakeholder engagement, not a single infographic-style deliverable — the method's value is in the underlying system theory it builds, not just the final image.
