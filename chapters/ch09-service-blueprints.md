# Chapter 9: Service Blueprints

## Core Idea
A service blueprint is a chronological, swim-lane diagram — pioneered by G. Lynn Shostack — that separates what the customer experiences (frontstage) from what the organization does to produce it (backstage), so that service delivery becomes systematic and diagnosable instead of accidental.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Service Blueprint** (G. Lynn Shostack, 1982/1984; structured and normalized by Mary Jo Bitner and colleagues): a swim-lane map of a service encounter built from five layers, top to bottom — **physical evidence** (tangible manifestations of touchpoints: devices, software, face-to-face materials), **customer actions** (the steps the customer takes), **onstage touchpoints** (provider actions visible to the customer), then the **line of visibility**, below which sit **backstage actions** (internal provision mechanisms invisible to the customer but directly shaping the experience) and **support processes** (internal processes, including interactions with partners or third-party suppliers, that only indirectly shape the experience). Shostack additionally marks **fail points** (an "F" in a black circle) — steps prone to breakdown. Use it to diagnose, improve, and manage an existing, well-defined service encounter, and to analyze exact timing of interactions down to the minute or second.
- **Frontstage / backstage** (theater metaphor, standard across service-design literature): frontstage is what the individual experiences; backstage is everything necessary to produce it but invisible to the customer, sitting below the line of visibility. This is service design's expression of the book's general value-alignment principle.
- **Lean Thinking's four steps** (James Womack & Daniel Jones): (1) **specify the value** — from the customer's perspective, for the whole experience, not individual interactions; (2) **identify the value chain** — all actions and processes needed to deliver that value, eliminating steps that don't add value; (3) **optimize flow** — increase the efficiency of backstage service processes; (4) **create customer pull** — once flow exists, let customer demand pull value upstream instead of pushing output at people. Do this before building a value stream map.
- **Value Stream Mapping** (the technique for lean step 2; explained by Karin Martin & Mike Osterling): diagrams the backstage value chain only — resembling the bottom half of a service blueprint, with no direct customer-experience content.
- **Lean Consumption / lean consumption maps** (Womack & Jones, "Lean Consumption," *HBR* 2005; illustrated by service designer Pete Abilla): map the steps and elapsed time a customer goes through to consume a product or service, typically as a before/after comparison of total customer time spent across every provider touched. Use when the goal is explicitly minimizing the customer's own time and effort cost, since off-loading work onto the customer looks efficient internally but destroys value.
- **Multi-actor service blueprinting** (Wreiner, Mårtensson, Arnell, Gonzalez, Holmlid & Segelström, 2009): extends the blueprint to show interactions between more than one provider-side actor — e.g., motorist, lot operator, and lot owner in a parking scenario — within a single map.
- **Expressive Service Blueprinting** (Susan Spraragen, with Carrie Chan): adds a feelings dimension to the standard blueprint through three distinguishing components — emotive responses, layout, provider identity (full treatment in Worked Example below). Use it when a standard blueprint would hide the emotional driver behind a service outcome.
- **Polaine's emotionally-expanded, multi-actor blueprints** (Andy Polaine, inspired by Spraragen): combines emotional context with multiple simultaneous actors in one diagram. The author notes this "adds a level of detail that makes the diagram more complex and may not be useful in all situations."

## Key Concepts
- **Fail point**: a step in the service, marked with Shostack's "F" in a black circle, flagged as prone to inconsistency or breakdown so it can be addressed pre-emptively.
- **Line of visibility**: the boundary in a service blueprint separating onstage touchpoints (visible to the customer) from backstage actions (invisible to the customer).
- **Physical evidence**: the tangible manifestation of a touchpoint — a device, software, or face-to-face material — that the customer directly interacts with.
- **Onstage touchpoints**: provider actions visible to the customer, positioned above the line of visibility.
- **Backstage actions**: internal service-provision mechanisms, invisible to the customer, that directly impact the experience.
- **Support processes**: internal processes, including interactions with partners or third-party suppliers, that only indirectly impact the customer experience.
- **Lean**: an umbrella term whose uses share one thing in common — reducing waste.
- **Lean consumption**: Womack & Jones's extension of lean thinking from the organization's internal processes to the customer's own time and effort cost of consuming a product or service.

## Mental Models
- Use a service blueprint when the point of view is a specific, real-time, usually face-to-face service encounter that needs diagnosis or management — not for broad, ongoing behavior patterns.
- Think of the line of visibility as the edge of a theater stage: everything below it is invisible to the audience, but everything above it depends on it.
- Use a lean consumption map specifically to show how much of the customer's own time the service costs — a value stream map alone only shows the organization's side of the ledger.
- Reach for expressive service blueprinting when a standard blueprint's swim lanes would flatten out an emotional arc that is actually driving the outcome (confusion, anxiety) — not as a default replacement for the standard format.

## Anti-patterns
- **Taking "blueprint" literally**: the name implies architectural precision, but by the book's own assessment these diagrams "are more like flow diagrams than an architectural blueprint" — expecting blueprint-grade rigor sets the wrong bar for the artifact.
- **Treating the blueprint as a complete picture of the experience**: it lacks contextual and environmental cues (e.g., "noisy setting" or "great tasting food") — read alone, it can miss why an encounter actually succeeded or failed.
- **Saving organizational effort by off-loading work onto the customer**: looks efficient backstage, but lean consumption shows it wastes the customer's time and erodes loyalty and revenue rather than protecting them.
- **Defaulting to maximum complexity** (multiple actors plus an emotive layer, as in Polaine's diagrams): adds real overhead and "may not be useful in all situations" — match the blueprint's complexity to the question being asked, not to the most feature-complete example available.

## Reference Tables
Table 9-1: Defining aspects of service blueprints (using the point-of-view/structure/scope/focus/uses/strengths/weaknesses framework from Chapter 2)

| Dimension | Description |
|---|---|
| Point of View | Examples vary from specific service encounters with limited scope to overviews of a whole service ecosystem. Extensions of the technique incorporate multiple actors and relationships. |
| Structure | Chronological. |
| Scope | Individual service encounter in real time, usually face-to-face. Typically centered on one actor on the customer side, but may also include multiple actors. |
| Focus | Service provision processes, with emphasis on backstage action and touchpoints. Extensions of service blueprinting add emotive information. |
| Uses | Diagnosis, improvement, and management of existing service systems. Good for analyzing specific timings of service interactions, down to the minute in some cases. |
| Strengths | Simple, predefined structure with a clear focus of attention. Relatively light research and investigation needed. Suitable for co-creation with teams and stakeholders. Easy for others to understand from a single page. |
| Weaknesses | Narrow in scope compared to other alignment diagram types. Lacks many of the contextual, environmental cues of an experience (e.g., "noisy setting" or "great tasting food"). The blueprint metaphor is a misnomer: more like a flow diagram than an architectural blueprint. |

## Worked Example
**Case study: "Expressive Service Blueprint"** — by Susan Spraragen, with Carrie Chan.

Spraragen was researching a human-centered way to visualize service systems and found that business process modeling notations, data flow diagrams, and operational charts marginalized the consumer rather than making their motivations and emotions explicit. Shostack and Bitner's service blueprints, drawn from the consumer's perspective, were closer to what she wanted — but still read as generic swim-lane diagrams. She challenged collaborator Carrie Chan to "create a service blueprint with limited use of boxes and arrows" that would explicitly show the human emotions of a service encounter. The result, which Spraragen named **Expressive Service Blueprinting**, adds three components not found in a traditional blueprint:

1. **Emotive responses** — consumer emotions shown via icons, photographs, graphs, or similar elements, modulated in intensity through color or positioning to emulate how the consumer's emotion varies through the encounter; embedded directly in the onstage journey, alongside the consumer's own thoughts as they perform each action.
2. **Layout** — more space is allocated to the onstage customer journey than to the backstage area (inverting a standard blueprint's usual backstage emphasis), and moments of direct interaction with the provider are drawn as vertical arrows, one-way or bi-directional.
3. **Provider identity** — participants are named by their actual functional role in the service domain (e.g., "ophthalmic technician") rather than generic labels like "provider" and "consumer."

The illustrative example (Figure 9-10) maps a patient's visit to an ophthalmologist, built from interviews and observations with an ophthalmic technician who sees many patients daily. The underlying problem is prescription compliance. The blueprint tracks two emotive states across the visit: **distraction**, shown as a line graph with the patient's icon marking key moments, and **anxiety**, shown as a wave form. Distraction stays low during the eye exam and the meeting with the ophthalmologist, then spikes during the exchange of information about how to follow the prescribed medication regimen — precisely the moment the patient most needs to retain what they're told. Anxiety climbs in parallel, driven by the prospect of adding another medicine to a daily routine and its cost. The diagram also exposes backstage complexity: while this patient is in the office, the ophthalmologist is simultaneously handling other patients' concerns. The takeaway the diagram invites is procedural: prescription information is being delivered at exactly the moment the patient is least equipped, emotionally and cognitively, to absorb it — pointing directly at when and how that exchange should be redesigned.

## Key Takeaways
1. Structure a service blueprint around its five layers — physical evidence, customer actions, onstage touchpoints, backstage actions, support processes — with the line of visibility drawn between onstage touchpoints and backstage actions.
2. Mark fail points explicitly (Shostack's "F" convention): the purpose of blueprinting is finding where a service can break down ahead of time, not just documenting the happy path.
3. Apply lean's four steps in order — specify value, identify the value chain, optimize flow, create customer pull — before drawing a value stream map of the backstage.
4. When the deliverable concerns the customer directly, build a lean consumption map and count the customer's own time cost across every provider touched, not only the organization's internal cost.
5. Reach for expressive service blueprinting specifically when a standard blueprint would hide the emotional driver of a bad outcome; its three additions (emotive responses, inverted layout, named provider identity) are worth the extra complexity only then.
6. Scope multi-actor and emotive extensions (Wreiner, Polaine) to cases a single-actor blueprint can't answer — they add real complexity and are not a default upgrade.
7. Pair a service blueprint with a broader diagram type when contextual or environmental cues matter: the format's own declared weakness is that it omits ambient detail like "noisy setting" or "great tasting food."
