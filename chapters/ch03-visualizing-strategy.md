# Chapter 3: Visualizing Strategy

## Core Idea
Organizations mature into **strategy myopia** — losing sight of the human need they actually serve and mistaking it for their current product or category — and alignment diagrams correct this by inverting the flow of insight: start from the individual's experience and work backward to technology and offerings, rather than pushing existing products outward. This chapter connects that outside-in view to established strategy tools (strategy maps, the strategy canvas, the strategy blueprint, the Business Model Canvas, the Value Proposition Canvas) so experience diagrams function as strategic input, not just execution documentation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four aspects of expanding the strategic field of vision** (Kalbach): a numbered checklist for correcting strategy myopia — (1) Reframe Competition: define rivals by what gets the customer's job done, not by industry category; (2) Create Shared Value: link revenue to social benefit via Porter's three levers; (3) Reimagine Value Delivery: account for the offering's place in a larger connected-device ecosystem; (4) Organize for Innovation: structure the org so new ideas survive contact with the existing business. Use as a diagnostic pass over an existing strategy, informed at each step by alignment-diagram findings.
- **Playing to Win** (A.G. Lafley & Roger Martin): five cascading strategy questions — What is your winning aspiration? Where will you play? How will you win? What capabilities are needed? How will you manage strategy? Kalbach uses this as one of two source frameworks (with Mintzberg's Five Ps) synthesized into the strategy blueprint.
- **Strategy Safari's Five Ps** (Henry Mintzberg): five lenses on the word "strategy" — Pattern, Position, Perspective, Ploy, Plan. Combined with Lafley & Martin's five questions (Table 3-1) to derive the strategy blueprint's six elements.
- **Strategy Map** (Robert Kaplan & David Norton, extending their Balanced Scorecard): a single-page strategy visualization stacking four perspectives — Learning and growth of employees, Internal processes, Customers, Financials — connected bottom-to-top as a chain of if-then causal statements. Use to show how one team's activities causally roll up into financial results; alignment diagrams supply evidence for the Customers row (what customers actually perceive as valuable).
- **Strategy Canvas / Blue Ocean Strategy** (W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne): a plot with factors of competition on the x-axis and relative performance (low to high) on the y-axis, one line per competitor. Distinguishes red oceans (crowded markets, rivals fighting over the same factors, shrinking share) from blue oceans (uncontested space, demand created rather than fought over). Build it in three steps: (1) determine factors of value creation — alignment diagrams help identify which matter; (2) determine competitor types, ideally three, never more than four; (3) rate performance per factor, on a relative scale or with empirical evidence such as a survey. Variant: derive the factors from customer-journey phases instead of industry-standard attributes, yielding an experience-based strategy canvas.
- **Strategy Blueprint** (Kalbach's own tool): a canvas of six elements — Challenges, Aspirations, Focus areas, Guiding principles, Activities, Measurements — synthesized from Playing to Win and the Five Ps. Use as a low-risk, non-linear exploration surface (briefing, workshop, reference document) for the logic connecting analysis to planning; no prescribed fill order, though starting with Challenges and Aspirations is recommended.
- **Business Model Canvas** (Alexander Osterwalder, *Business Model Generation*): a nine-box canvas splitting a business model into front stage (market-facing elements, right side) and back stage (internal processes, left side); used to sketch, compare, and pressure-test alternative business models — e.g., on sticky notes in a workshop — before committing to one.
- **Value Proposition Canvas** (Alexander Osterwalder / Strategyzer): a two-sided zoom-in that plugs into the Business Model Canvas's customer-segments and value-proposition boxes, matching a customer profile against a value map to test the fit between what's offered and what's wanted.

## Key Concepts
- **Strategy myopia**: an organization's narrowed view of what business it is actually in, mistaking a product or technology category for the underlying human need it serves.
- **Outside-in vs. inside-out**: building strategy starting from the customer's experience and working back to technology and offerings, versus starting from existing capabilities and pushing them outward.
- **Jobs to be done (as segmentation)**: segmenting markets by the outcome or job a person is trying to accomplish rather than by demographic or psychographic attributes; determines who counts as a competitor.
- **Arenas** (Rita Gunther McGrath): market categories defined by the experiences people have and their connection to a provider, replacing industry/product categorization as the unit of competitive analysis.
- **Shared value** (Michael Porter): linking revenue generation directly to producing social benefit, distinct from corporate social responsibility layered on top of a separately profit-maximizing core.
- **Air Sandwich** (Nilofer Merchant): a strategy with a clear vision on top and daily action on the bottom but no connecting layer of decisions in the middle, leaving execution unaligned with intent.
- **Ambidextrous organization** (Charles O'Reilly & Michael Tushman): structurally separating divisions that protect and exploit existing revenue from divisions that explore new ideas, so incumbency doesn't strangle the new.
- **Red ocean / blue ocean**: red = existing, crowded market space with fierce head-to-head competition on the same factors; blue = uncontested market space where demand is created rather than fought over.
- **Front stage / back stage** (Business Model Canvas): the canvas's right-side market-facing components versus its left-side internal-process components.

## Mental Models
- Use the **strategy canvas** when you want to diagnose how your value creation compares to rivals on shared factors, or to find uncontested space by dropping factors rivals compete on and investing in ones they ignore.
- Use the **strategy blueprint** when you need one non-linear canvas to work out the qualitative logic connecting market/financial analysis to a detailed roadmap — strategy is the reasoning between the two, not either one alone.
- Think of the **Business Model Canvas** as a viability-testing surface: move sticky notes to try alternative business models before committing to one, the same way a strategy canvas tries alternative competitive positions.
- Think of alignment diagrams as strategic inputs, not execution artifacts: they populate the Customers row of a strategy map, the factors of a strategy canvas, and the Aspirations/Focus-areas boxes of the strategy blueprint.

## Anti-patterns
- **Strategy myopia (Kodak)**: Kodak invented the first digital camera in 1975 but filed for bankruptcy in 2012 anyway — not from missing the technology, but from believing it was in the film business rather than the storytelling business, and defending the existing business through marketing and sales instead of confronting the shift. Levitt's railroad-industry example is the same failure: railroads saw themselves as in the railroad business, not the transportation business, and lost growth to cars, trucks, planes, and telephones despite no head-to-head product competing directly.
- **Push instead of pull**: treating customers as a market to be "wrung" for what they're worth (the chapter's opening anecdote) rather than inverting the flow of insight to start from what customers want — a posture that trades sustained growth for short-term extraction.
- **Demographic/psychographic segmentation**: categorizing customers by age, income, marital status, or company size doesn't match actual needs and motivations, producing a one-size-fits-all approach that inevitably fails and forces arbitrary reshuffling of categories.
- **The Air Sandwich**: communicating strategy as a top-down PowerPoint reveal with no shared middle layer of decisions connecting vision to daily action, then blaming "poor execution" when alignment never happens.
- **Darwinistic view of innovation**: assuming the best new ideas will rise to the surface on merit alone, ignoring that "corporate antibodies" motivated to protect existing revenue will summarily squash fledgling ideas unless the organization is structurally set up to accept them.

## Worked Example
Applying the strategy canvas to the US airline industry (Kim & Mauborgne's Southwest Airlines case, as Kalbach walks through it):

1. **Determine factors of value creation.** The traditional factors airlines compete on include things like meals, lounges, seating choice, and hub connections — the standard menu of "airline service."
2. **Determine competitor types.** Plot a small set of representative carriers — Kalbach's rule of thumb is three, never more than four, since additional lines dilute the picture.
3. **Rate performance per factor.** Each carrier gets a relative low-to-high score on every factor, plotted as a line across the canvas.

Southwest's line diverges sharply from the rest of the industry: it deliberately scores low or absent on many traditional factors and instead invests almost entirely in one — frequent departures from smaller, secondary airports. That single tradeoff moves Southwest out of the red ocean of airlines fighting over the same factors (meals, lounges, hubs) and into uncontested space: because departures are frequent and airports are close-in, Southwest ends up competing with car travel, not just other airlines — travelers who would have driven between two cities now consider flying instead. This is Blue Ocean Strategy's core move in action: don't out-compete rivals on their factors, make the comparison irrelevant by changing which factors matter.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat "what business are we in" as a live question, defined by the customer's job or need (storytelling, transportation) rather than by the current product or technology category, to avoid strategy myopia.
2. Segment markets by jobs to be done and desired outcomes, not demographics — demographics don't predict purchase motivation and lead to arbitrary, recurring re-segmentation.
3. When building a strategy canvas, cap competitors at about three and derive the factors of value creation from alignment-diagram findings (e.g., customer journey phases) rather than guessing at industry standards.
4. Use the strategy blueprint's six elements as a single non-linear canvas synthesizing Playing to Win and Mintzberg's Five Ps; start with Challenges and Aspirations, then move freely between boxes.
5. Feed alignment-diagram insight into the Customers perspective of a Kaplan & Norton strategy map — it's the evidence for what customers actually perceive as valuable, one link in the map's causal chain up to Financials.
6. To act on shared value, identify which of Porter's three levers applies — reconceive the offering, change how it's produced, or change how you collaborate with partners — then locate it on the experience diagram at a real customer moment.
7. Structurally separate "protect the core" from "explore the new" (ambidextrous organization), and where possible organize teams around stages of the customer journey rather than function or technology, so no single group is the sole owner of "the customer."
