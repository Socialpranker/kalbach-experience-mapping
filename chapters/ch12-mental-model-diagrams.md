# Chapter 12: Mental Model Diagrams

## Core Idea
A mental model diagram makes a person's thinking — the reasoning, reactions, and guiding principles behind how they pursue a purpose — visible and hierarchical, then aligns an organization's offerings underneath it; mapping experiences, in this view, is fundamentally mapping someone's mental model.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Mental model diagram (Indi Young)**: a hierarchical, two-part diagram split by a dark center line. Top half — the mental model itself — has three nested levels: **Boxes** (smallest unit of analysis: one thinking process, reaction, or guiding principle per box; originally called "tasks" by Young, later dropped to avoid implying physical action only), grouped by affinity into **Towers** (colored-background column clusters), grouped again into **Mental spaces** (labels spanning several towers — a person's broader head-spaces). Bottom half — **Support** — is everything the organization offers (products, services, features) mapped underneath each tower to show what currently addresses that thinking. Use it to study a person pursuing a purpose broader than your product, independent of any particular system.
- **Don Norman's three-model framework** (*The Design of Everyday Things*, 1988): the designer's model of the system, the actual system model, and the user's mental model of the system are three distinct things, connected only by a feedback loop. Design's job is closing that loop through empathy — setting aside your own (maker's) perspective to view the system as the user does.
- **Element-writing format**: every box is summarized as **[I (optional)] + [verb] + [noun] + [qualifiers]** — start with a verb to capture thinking rather than a goal, write in first person to stand in the participant's shoes, one idea per box. This uniformity is what makes later grouping into towers and mental spaces tractable.
- **Combing the transcripts**: the research method behind the diagram — comb listening-session transcripts for quotes on a single concept, tag each as thinking / reaction / guiding principle, then normalize into the verb-first format above before affinity-grouping.
- **Rapid Mental Model Method / "Lightning Quick Method"** (Young, blog post, 2010): a days-not-months alternative run as one extended workshop. (1) *Solicit stories in advance* — collect short first-person stories (1-2 pages) on the topic a week out, by email or social media. (2) *Comb & summarize* — read stories aloud in the workshop; team members write summaries in parallel (roughly 100 in a few hours). (3) *Group by patterns* — cluster summaries into towers, then mental spaces, live (a provisional structure within an afternoon). (4) *Brainstorm* — use remaining workshop time to ideate against the gaps, on a separate sticky-note row below the provisional diagram. Faster and still grounded in real stories, but shallower than the full method and may need further validation.
- **From Construct to Structure**: a grounded, bottom-up process for deriving information architecture — group summaries into towers and towers into mental spaces, then reuse that resulting categorization directly as site/app navigation, since it already matches the vocabulary people actually used.

## Key Concepts
- **Mental model**: a person's cognitive construct — built from beliefs, assumptions, and past experience — of how something works; a *perception* of a system, not necessarily an accurate account of how the system actually works. The term originates in psychology, formalized for design use by Kenneth Craik (1943) and Philip Johnson-Laird (*Mental Models*, 1983) before Young's technique.
- **Box**: the smallest unit in a mental model diagram — a single thinking process, reaction, or guiding principle.
- **Tower**: an affinity group of boxes, rendered as a colored column.
- **Mental space**: an affinity cluster of towers — a person's broader head-space, labeled above the towers it spans.
- **Support**: Young's term for everything below the center line — the organization's products, services, and features, mapped to the thinking they address.
- **Laddering**: visualizing layers of causality from granular evidence up to high-level conclusions; mental model diagrams (and the related goals-means framework) are a species of laddering.
- **Grounded**: a bottom-up process — structure derived from real research data rather than imposed top-down.

## Mental Models
- Think of the thermostat as **a switch, not a faucet valve**: turning it up further doesn't push out heat faster, it just keeps the heater running longer to reach a higher target — the book's opening illustration of a wrong mental model, and of why a maker's system knowledge and a user's mental model routinely diverge.
- Use a mental model diagram to study **a person pursuing a purpose**, not **a user operating your system** — banning tool, software, and hardware references from the top half is what breaks the diagram out of the "system frame" and lets it apply to any actor in an ecosystem (the book illustrates this with a mental model diagram built for a cat).
- Treat an opinion or preference surfaced in research as **shorthand for underlying thinking, not as data itself** — dig until you find the reasoning that produced the stance, and record that reasoning instead.
- Think of the finished mental model as **foundational, not disposable**: because it captures human reasoning with no built-in solution, it changes slowly and often stays relevant for years, unlike a diagram tied to a specific product generation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Writing boxes around tools or software** (e.g., "Filter image colors in Photoshop"): breaks the person-focus that gives the diagram its longevity. The correct box is the root task — "Alter image colors" — with Photoshop and its filters recorded only in Support, below the line.
- **Recording opinions or preferences verbatim as boxes**: an opinion is shorthand for a stance that "can be widely interpreted," not the underlying thought — skipping the step of asking what thinking produced it loses the actual unit of analysis.
- **Assuming your model of the system matches the user's**: the maker always understands the system better than the user does; without an explicit empathy/feedback loop (Norman's triad), that gap silently produces mismatched designs — exactly what happens when someone treats a thermostat like a faucet.
- **Presenting the full, detailed diagram to time-pressed stakeholders with no simplified rollup**: the level of detail that makes mental model diagrams valuable for deep understanding is the same thing that makes senior leaders wave off the presentation and ask for something simpler — a reaction the author has witnessed directly.

## Worked Example
Indi Young's case study for a disguised insurance company. A strategy group — separate from the auto- and home-insurance business lines — had already studied what goes through people's minds during and after an auto accident, and wanted a companion study on **near-misses** to widen the foundation for new-product thinking. Research scope, deliberately not restricted to cars or to the insurance product itself: *"What was on your mind during and after a memorable near-miss accident?"*

1. **Listening sessions.** 24 people, opened with that scope question, then followed wherever the participant went. Example: a driver recounts a bracket falling off a truck ahead and cracking her windshield at 65 mph; the listener probes the word "adrenaline," her decision not to report it, and her husband's reaction — surfacing reasoning and emotion, not just the event.
2. **Writing summaries.** For each transcript, the team strung together scattered quotes on one concept, tagged it thinking/reaction/guiding-principle, tried candidate verbs, then wrote the normalized summary — e.g., quotes about looking up a truck company's license plate became "Identify who made this accident happen... because I want to know who did it."
3. **Finding patterns.** Summaries from all 24 transcripts were piled by affinity, then the piles themselves grouped a second time into ten higher-level patterns (e.g., "Recognize I am in a dangerous situation," "Get safe again," "Follow the insurance process," "Try to prevent this from happening again/to others"), each with lettered sub-piles.
4. **Building the diagram.** Pile labels became tower titles; individual summaries became boxes; the ten higher-level groups became mental spaces.
5. **Zeroing in on business goals.** Against five standing yearly goals (increase membership, reduce claims, leverage social capital, expand mobile services, increase employee pride), the team flagged the patterns that looked most actionable — e.g., "Report the incident... so they know what happened."
6. **Inspiring ideas.** Two examples from stakeholder ideation: **"Warn Others"** — let customers flag a hazard so the company routes the warning through channels like traffic apps (mapped to Reduce Claims, Build Social Capital, Increase Membership); **"Claim-Lite"** — a lighter claims path for incidents people themselves frame as "it could have been so much worse" (mapped to Increase Membership and Reduce Claims). Not every idea proceeds — the team treats empathic understanding from the diagram as the filter for which ideas earn further investment.

## Key Takeaways
1. Split the diagram hard at the center line: mental model (top, person-focused, tool-free) above, Support (bottom, your offerings) below — that split is the alignment mechanism itself.
2. Write every element verb-first, first-person, one-idea-per-box; the normalized format is what makes later grouping into towers and mental spaces possible at all.
3. Keep software, hardware, and brand names out of the top half — their presence signals you've drifted from the person's thinking into your own system's frame.
4. When research surfaces an opinion or preference, keep probing for the reasoning behind it; the stance itself isn't the unit of analysis.
5. Match method to timeline: full projects run 20-30 participants over weeks/months and print 10-15 feet wide; reach for Young's Lightning Quick Method (solicit stories → comb & summarize → group by patterns → brainstorm) when you need something grounded but fast.
6. Reuse the bottom-up box→tower→mental-space categorization directly as information architecture or navigation — it already matches users' real vocabulary and will outlast an org-derived IA.
7. Prepare a simplified rollup alongside the full diagram — stakeholders under time pressure often reject the level of detail that makes the diagram valuable in the first place.
