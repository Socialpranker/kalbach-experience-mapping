# Chapter 2: Fundamentals of Mapping Experiences

## Core Idea
Experience is holistic, personal, and situational, so mapping it is an act of deliberate selection: the mapmaker frames what the diagram is about (point of view, scope, focus, structure, use), inventories touchpoints, and singles out the emotionally charged Moments of Truth — all in service of understanding value, including Jobs To Be Done — rather than producing a complete or "objective" artifact.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Transitional volatility** (David Danielson, 2003): the degree of reorientation a person feels moving from page to page (or touchpoint to touchpoint). Pattern: habituate → predict → reorient, repeating. High volatility comes from inconsistency across touchpoints. Use mapping to locate where volatility is highest and reduce unnecessary reorientation — not to force uniformity, but coherency.
- **Frame the Mapping Effort — five dimensions**: Point of View (whose experience, which type of experience — answers "what is it about?"), Scope (breadth vs. depth trade-off, boundaries and granularity), Focus (which individual- and organization-side elements come to the foreground), Structure (chronological / hierarchical / spatial / network), and Use (who reads it, what questions it must answer, how it will be used). Do all five explicitly, up front, before diagramming.
- **Touchpoint types**: Static (no interaction — ads, newsletters), Interactive (websites, apps, chat), Human (person-to-person — sales reps, support agents). A **channel** is not a touchpoint but a category of touchpoint by mode of delivery (e.g., point-of-sale, one-to-one, indirect, mass communication).
- **Diagram structure types**: Chronological (timeline, most common — service blueprints, customer journey maps, experience maps), Hierarchical (removes time; for many simultaneous aspects — mental model diagrams), Spatial (physical or metaphorical 3D space — spatial maps), Network (web of interrelationships, neither chronological nor hierarchical, e.g., mapping which touchpoints drive positive/negative experience).
- **Moments of Truth** (Jan Carlzon): critical, emotionally charged touchpoints that occur when someone has invested high energy in a desired outcome; they make or break the relationship. Overall perception of an organization = the sum of all Moments of Truth.
- **Zero Moment of Truth / ZMOT** (Google researchers, Jim Lecinski): a fourth touchpoint added to the classic Stimulus → First Moment of Truth (decision to buy) → Second Moment of Truth (first use) sequence. ZMOT sits between Stimulus and the buying decision: online research, reviews, and social opinion-seeking before purchase. Design implication: content at ZMOT must be genuinely valuable, not marketing fluff, positioning the company as a trusted advisor.
- **Five types of customer value** (Sheth, Newman & Gross): Functional (utilitarian performance/reliability), Social (interaction among people, lifestyle), Emotional (feelings/affective response), Epistemic (curiosity, learning), Conditional (value that depends on situation/context).
- **Premium value / 15 types of meaning** (Nathan Shedroff, with Steve Diller & Darrel Rhea, *Making Meaning*): Accomplishment, Beauty, Community, Creation, Duty, Enlightenment, Freedom, Harmony, Justice, Oneness, Redemption, Security, Truth, Validation, Wonder — value beyond novelty/delight, tied to personal meaning and identity.
- **Jobs To Be Done** (Clayton Christensen, *The Innovator's Solution*): people "hire" products/services to get a job done. Every job has three dimensions — Functional (practical task), Emotional (desired feelings while doing it), Social (how one believes one will be perceived). The unit of analysis is the customer's *circumstance*, not the customer's demographic/psychographic profile.
- **Opportunity Score** (Tony Ulwick): for each desired-outcome statement, survey respondents rate Importance and Satisfaction (1–10 scale); Opportunity Score = Importance + (Importance − Satisfaction). High-importance/low-satisfaction statements are the best innovation targets. Deliberately measures customer opportunity, not financial or market-size opportunity.

## Key Concepts
- **Transitional volatility**: the degree of reorientation a person experiences moving between touchpoints; high volatility = disjointed experience.
- **Touchpoint**: the means of value exchange between an individual and an organization; broader definitions (Risdon, Walters) frame it as "a point of interaction involving a specific human need in a specific time and place," not just a company-defined channel.
- **Moments of Truth**: critical, emotionally charged touchpoints where high personal investment meets a desired outcome, making or breaking the relationship.
- **Zero Moment of Truth (ZMOT)**: the pre-purchase research/review phase, inserted between Stimulus and the First Moment of Truth.
- **Jobs To Be Done**: the underlying functional, emotional, and social outcome a person seeks by "hiring" a solution — not a task list or use-case inventory.
- **Point of view**: the combination of "whose experience" and "which type of experience," giving a diagram its single common thread.
- **Premium value**: meaning-based value (per Shedroff) that goes beyond novelty and delight to give the person identity and sense-making.
- **Opportunity score**: Ulwick's importance-plus-satisfaction-gap formula for ranking unmet Jobs To Be Done.
- **Channel**: a category of touchpoint defined by mode of delivery, distinct from the touchpoint itself.

## Mental Models
- Use a **customer journey map** when the point of view is purchasing behavior and the goal is optimizing sales.
- Use a **mental model diagram** when the point of view is broader consumption/behavior patterns and the goal is expanding the offering.
- Use an **experience map** when the point of view is a day-in-the-life across a person's daily routine.
- Think of touchpoints as **building blocks of a system of experiences** — the design challenge is the *connections* between touchpoints, not each touchpoint in isolation ("the system is the experience," per Brugnoli).
- Use a **hierarchical structure** instead of chronological when many aspects occur simultaneously and are hard to force into a single timeline.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating touchpoint inventories as sufficient**: a company-organized list (often mirroring the org chart) assumes a linear, direct relationship and that customers engage meaningfully with each item — usually false, and entirely company-focused rather than need-focused (per Risdon and Walters).
- **Trying to design or control every touchpoint**: futile — some aspects lie beyond the organization's control; the job is to know which ones to focus on and how to limit damage on the rest.
- **Mixing multiple points of view in one diagram without saying so**: weakens the message; a single clear perspective per diagram is the default, and combinations must be made explicit.
- **Treating Jobs To Be Done as task analysis or a use-case list**: a fallacy — the functional job is only one of three dimensions (with emotional and social), and the real target is the underlying need and desired outcome.
- **Targeting customers instead of circumstances**: Christensen's critique — products aimed at customer demographics rather than the circumstances customers find themselves in are less predictably successful.
- **Conflating opportunity score with financial/market-size opportunity**: Ulwick's score is intentionally a customer-adoption measure only; using it as a business-case number misreads the method.

## Worked Example
**Case study: "Identifying Opportunities: Combining Mental Model Diagrams and Jobs To Be Done"** — by Jim Kalbach with Jen Padilla, Elizabeth Thapliyal, Ryan Kasper (Citrix GoToMeeting UX team).

Problem: product development needed a solid, needs-based theory to connect user insight to prioritization decisions, rather than ad hoc feature requests. Six-step process:

1. **Conduct primary research.** Contextual inquiry across the domain of work collaboration/communication: 40+ on-site interviews with stakeholders and team members; field notes, photos, audio, and video; 68 hours of audio transcribed by a third party into ~1,500 pages of text.
2. **Create a Mental Model Diagram.** Following Indi Young's method, the team analyzed transcripts for the jobs people were trying to get done, bottom-up: individual findings clustered into themes, themes grouped into categories, producing a "work collaboration" mental model diagram grounded directly in field data. Existing products/features were mapped underneath to show how current offerings did or didn't support each need.
3. **Hold a workshop.** ~12 stakeholders from various departments split into breakout groups, each covering roughly a third of the diagram. Step one: empathize with the current user experience by reading the diagram. Step two: brainstorm against "future of work" industry-trend scenarios, prompted per section with "If each trend came true, what must we do to support customers and evolve as a company?" Output was distilled into a laminated one-page infographic mailed to participants — still visible on desks a year later.
4. **Map concepts to the diagram.** The diagram was updated with workshop input and extended into three vertical layers below the user's mental-model needs: current support ("support towers") in the middle, future concepts/innovations at the bottom.
5. **Prioritize Jobs To Be Done.** Using Tony Ulwick's technique: derived ~30 desired-outcome statements directly from the mental model diagram; ran a quantitative survey rating each for importance and satisfaction; computed Opportunity Score = importance + (importance − satisfaction) (example: importance 9, satisfaction 3 → score 15). High-importance/low-satisfaction jobs mark the highest-adoption-potential opportunities. The score was deliberately scoped to customer opportunity only, not financial or market-size opportunity.
6. **Focus innovation efforts.** Four layers were visually aligned on the extended diagram: (1) the individual's experience as a mental model, (2) current supporting services, (3) team-proposed future concepts, (4) areas of unmet need from the opportunity scores. Prioritization against this combined picture gave the team confidence the roadmap was grounded in primary research.

Outcome: multiple concepts prototyped; two innovations shipped to the Apple Store plus several patent submissions; product managers, marketing managers, and engineers all used the prioritized needs list as a shared, consumable artifact for decision-making.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do the framing work before drawing anything: fix point of view, scope, focus, structure, and intended use, and state the trade-offs explicitly rather than letting them default.
2. Keep one point of view per diagram — one audience, one type of experience — for a clear message; if you need several, make the combination explicit rather than implicit.
3. Define touchpoints by the person's need-in-context (time and place), not only by company channel — an org-chart-shaped touchpoint inventory misses how people actually experience the system.
4. Prioritize design and innovation effort on Moments of Truth — the emotionally charged points that make or break the relationship — rather than spreading effort evenly across all touchpoints.
5. Account for the Zero Moment of Truth: build valuable, non-promotional content for the pre-purchase research phase, since customers now investigate extensively before the first moment of truth.
6. Define Jobs To Be Done across all three dimensions (functional, emotional, social) and target the customer's circumstance, not a demographic segment — a functional task list alone is not JTBD.
7. Pair a bottom-up needs map (mental model diagram) with a quantitative prioritization method (JTBD opportunity scoring) to turn qualitative research into a defensible, shared innovation roadmap.
